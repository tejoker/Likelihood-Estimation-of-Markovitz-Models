This section describes the S\&P~500 data of E1 and E3--E5 and the generators evaluated in E3
(univariate) and E4 (multivariate), and compares the stylized facts of real and synthetic
returns. The windowing and scaling of \cref{sec:method-setup} apply throughout; details of the
data construction and further fit diagnostics are in \cref{app:data}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The S\texorpdfstring{\&}{and}P~500 panel}\label{sec:data-panel}

The raw data are daily closing prices (Bloomberg \texttt{PX\_LAST}) from 2018-01-02 to
2025-06-16 of the 503 stocks that were constituents of the S\&P~500 when the file was extracted,
at the end of the sample. The prices are adjusted for splits and spin-offs but not for cash
dividends: no retained ticker has a split-sized return, the prices of MMM and GE show no drop at
their spin-offs during \TEST{}, and the prices of a dividend payer such as MSFT are its unadjusted
closes. The returns are therefore price returns. Omitting the dividend yield hardly changes
volatilities, but it lowers mean returns, which enter the \TRAIN{} means of the mean--variance
rules of E4 and the levels of their certainty equivalents and Sharpe ratios
(\cref{sec:mk-design}).

Missing prices are never back-filled:
a ticker enters the complete universe if it has a price on the first and on the last trading day
and no internal gap longer than three days. The 22 tickers whose history starts later fail this
rule; none of the remaining 481 has a missing price. A rule-based screen then flags a ticker if
one daily log-return exceeds 1 in absolute value or if it has at least 20 days with a price but
zero volume. It flags exactly two data errors: KDP, whose price is not adjusted for a special
dividend (one log-return of $-1.718$), and AMCR, a stale quote before its listing in June 2019.
The default universe therefore has 479 tickers (\cref{tab:data-universe,tab:data-anomalies}).
The main panel of E1, E3 and E5 consists of $N=100$ tickers drawn from it with a fixed seed
(\cref{tab:data-panel}); E4 draws 30 universes of 30 names from the same 479
(\cref{sec:mk-design}).

\TRAIN{} holds the $\Ttr=1258$ daily log-returns from 2018-01-03 to 2022-12-30 (the price of
2018-01-02 is the base of the first one) and \TEST{} the $\Tte=615$ returns from 2023-01-03 to
2025-06-16. At $L=20$ the panel gives 6200 \TRAIN{} and 3000 \TEST{} windows; at $L=5$, used by
the per-ticker tests of E3 and by E4, a ticker gives 123 \TEST{} windows. E5 also uses an
alternative split at 2021-12-31 (\cref{sec:robust}).

Index membership is that of the extraction date, at the end of \TEST{}, and a ticker must also
have traded throughout the sample. Firms that were delisted, acquired or removed from the index
after 2018 are absent, so the panel is subject to survivorship bias
\citep{brown1992survivorship,shumway1997delisting}. The selection goes further than survivorship:
both \TRAIN{} and \TEST{} are conditioned on membership at the end of \TEST{}, which a forecaster
at the end of \TRAIN{} could not know. Firms removed from the index during \TEST{} are absent and
firms added during \TEST{} are present. SMCI and DECK, for instance, entered the index during
\TEST{} in place of WHR and ZION; the first two belong to the 479-ticker universe (SMCI enters
three E4 universes), the last two are not in the file. Deletions typically follow losses
and high volatility, so their absence probably lowers the volatility of the real \TEST{} returns
relative to the population faced by an investor at the end of 2022; additions after strong price
rises, such as SMCI, work in the other direction, and our data cannot sign the net effect. The
selection also biases mean returns upward, in \TRAIN{} in particular, where they enter the
mean--variance rules of E4. Part of the gap between the volatility of the generators, all
calibrated on \TRAIN{}, and that of the real \TEST{} returns (\cref{sec:data-stylized}) may
therefore be a selection effect, and the verdicts of \cref{sec:emp} concern this population only.
The export contains no point-in-time membership, so we cannot count the tickers of the panel that
were not constituents at the end of \TRAIN{}; a replication should draw the universe from the
constituents at that date.

\subsection{Generators}\label{sec:data-generators}

\paragraph{Univariate generators (E3, E5).}
Each generator is fitted to the raw \TRAIN{} returns of each ticker separately, in daily units
under the physical measure. A synthetic sample is one path of \TEST{} length per ticker; it is
divided by $s_i$, cut into windows and given the \TEST{} date ranks, exactly like the real
\TEST{} windows. \GARCHt{} and Heston start from the fitted end-of-\TRAIN{} state, the other
generators are stationary. E3 draws five synthetic seeds; seed 0 is the headline path.

\emph{GBM} has \iid{} $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ returns, estimated in closed form. \emph{Merton}
\citep{merton1976option} adds compound Poisson jumps: $r_t=\mu+\sigma\varepsilon_t+\sum_{k\le
N_t}J_k$ with $\varepsilon_t\sim N(0,1)$, $N_t\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_J)$ and
$J_k\sim N(\mu_J,\sigma_J^2)$, all independent; its likelihood, a Poisson mixture of Gaussians
truncated after at least ten terms and where the omitted Poisson mass falls below $10^{-12}$, is
maximised from several starting points with $\lambda_J\le0.5$ per day. \emph{\GARCHt{}}
\citep{bollerslev1986generalized,bollerslev1987conditionally} has $r_t=\mu+\sigma_tz_t$,
$\sigma_t^2=\omega+a(r_{t-1}-\mu)^2+b\sigma_{t-1}^2$ and $z_t$ \iid{} standardised
Student-$t_\nu$. We use variance targeting, $\omega=(1-a-b)\hat\sigma^2$ with $\hat\sigma^2$ the
\TRAIN{} sample variance \citep{engle_1996_garch,francq_2011_merits}, and maximise the likelihood
in $(\mu,a,b,\nu)$ subject to $a+b\le0.995$. On tickers with a volatility regime shift in 2020
the unrestricted estimator can drive $a+b$ to the stationarity boundary, where
$\omega/(1-a-b)$ is poorly identified and simulated paths can explode; with targeting the
unconditional variance equals the sample variance, and the cap keeps the model away from the
boundary. The model is symmetric: the asymmetric GARCH models that are standard for equity
returns \citep{nelson_1991_conditional,glosten_1993_relation} were not evaluated, so \GARCHt{}
has no leverage effect (\cref{sec:data-stylized}), and statements that \GARCHt{} is the best
parametric generator refer to the generators of this section only.

\emph{Heston} \citep{heston1993closed} has $d\log S_t=(\mu-v_t/2)\,dt+\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_{1t}$ and
$dv_t=\kappa_v(\theta_v-v_t)\,dt+\xi_v\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_{2t}$, $d\langle W_1,W_2\rangle_t=\rho_v\,dt$.
We simulate it with a daily step of the quadratic-exponential (QE) scheme of
\citet{andersen_2008_simple} and bound the coefficient of variation of the stationary variance,
$c=\xi_v/\sqrt{2\kappa_v\theta_v}$, by $\sqrt{1.5}\approx1.22$. Under this bound QE stays in its
quadratic branch and the variance is positive on every simulated day (\cref{app:data}); above
it, the exponential branch puts a point mass at zero and, as with a full-truncation Euler step
\citep{lord2010comparison}, many simulated days have zero variance and a constant return. The
bound is an artefact of the daily discretisation, not a restriction of the Heston model, in
which the variance integrated over a day is positive for every $c$. With sub-daily QE steps the
point mass at zero would affect short sub-steps rather than whole days, so the bound could be
relaxed; our implementation uses a daily step, and the Heston generator evaluated in this paper,
with its verdicts, is the Heston model restricted to $c\le\sqrt{1.5}$. The parameters are
calibrated by the simulated method of
moments \citep{duffie1993simulated}, with common random numbers, on the variance, the tail
quantile ratio $(q_{0.99}-q_{0.01})/(q_{0.75}-q_{0.25})$, the autocorrelations of $r_t^2$ at lags
1, 5, 10 and 20, and $\Corr(r_t,r_{t+1}^2)$; $\mu$ matches the sample mean. As $v_t$ is latent,
simulation starts from an exponentially weighted moving average (decay 0.94) of squared returns
at the end of \TRAIN{}.

The \emph{stationary bootstrap} \citep{politis1994stationary} resamples the \TRAIN{} returns in
blocks of geometric length. Its mean block length is the rule of \citet{politis2004automatic},
with the correction of \citet{patton2009correction}, applied to $\abs{r_t-\bar r}$: applied to
the nearly uncorrelated returns themselves, the rule selects blocks of a few days, which destroy
volatility clustering. The bootstrap copies windows of $\Rpast$, so the relative tests are not
valid for it and it receives no verdict (\cref{sec:method-relative}). GBM, Merton and \GARCHt{}
have explicit likelihoods, the benchmarks of \cref{sec:emp-likelihood}; Heston and the bootstrap
have none. Fitting algorithms are detailed in \cref{app:impl}.

\paragraph{Multivariate generators (E4).}
Each is fitted to the \TRAIN{} returns of one 30-asset universe. \emph{MV Gaussian} draws \iid{}
vectors with the sample mean and covariance; \emph{MV Student-$t$} draws \iid{} multivariate
$t_\nu$ vectors fitted by ECME \citep{liu1995ml}. \emph{CCC-GARCH-$t$}
\citep{bollerslev1990modelling} couples the univariate \GARCHt{} margins by a Gaussian copula
estimated from the normal scores of the standardised residuals \citep{joe2005asymptotic}. The
copula keeps each fitted margin, with its own $\nu$, at the price of zero tail dependence; and
because each conditional variance responds only to the asset's own shocks, simulated returns are
less correlated than the data (\cref{tab:e4-correlation}). The \emph{MV bootstrap} resamples
whole \TRAIN{} days, with blocks shared by all assets and the median over assets of the
univariate block length. \emph{Factor GARCH-$t$} is a single-index model
\citep{sharpe_1963_simplified,engle_1990_asset}, $r_{it}=\mu_i+\beta_im_t+e_{it}$, whose factor
$m_t$ is the equal-weight return of the universe, with OLS loadings and independent \GARCHt{}
models for $m_t$ and for each residual, so that assets share the volatility regimes of the
factor; it has no closed-form likelihood because the factor is latent. Because the factor is the
mean of the same assets, the OLS residuals add up to zero every day, an identity that the
independent residual models do not reproduce (\cref{app:mk-generators}). Fitted to the 100-ticker
panel, the same model generates the simulated placebo panel of E1 (\cref{sec:emp-placebo}).

\paragraph{Fit diagnostics.}
\Cref{tab:e3-fit-diagnostics} summarises the univariate fits (full table in \cref{app:data}).
The Merton fits use jumps as a scale mixture for fat tails rather than as rare crashes: jumps are
frequent (median intensity 0.152 per day) and moderate (median jump standard deviation 3.73\%
against a median diffusion volatility of 1.38\%), so the verdicts on Merton concern this fitted
scale mixture, not jump models of rare crashes. The \GARCHt{} fits have median persistence 0.965 and
median $\nu=4.88$; one ticker reaches the cap. Heston is the hardest to fit: 33 tickers have a
parameter at a bound, mostly $\rho_v=-0.95$ or $c=\sqrt{1.5}$. Within the bound the calibration
matches the variance, the tail quantile ratio and the leverage moment, but it falls short of the
short-lag autocorrelation of $r_t^2$ and reaches a median kurtosis of 5.62 against 13.61 in the
data (\cref{tab:e3-heston-moments}): a diffusive variance with $c\le\sqrt{1.5}$ cannot produce
the few crash days that drive the sample kurtosis. Diagnostics of the multivariate fits are in
\cref{app:mk-generators}.

\subsection{Stylized facts}\label{sec:data-stylized}

\Cref{tab:e3-stylized} and \cref{fig:e3-stylized} compare the stylized facts
\citep{cont2001empirical} of the real \TEST{} returns with those of the headline synthetic path
of each generator. The first fact is non-stationarity. Every generator is more volatile than the
real \TEST{} returns (median daily standard deviation between 1.89\% and 2.13\%, against
1.62\%): each is calibrated to the volatility of \TRAIN{}, whose median is 2.11\% (the GBM
$\sigma$ in \cref{tab:e3-fit-diagnostics}), and part of the gap may come from the selection of
the universe on membership at the end of \TEST{} (\cref{sec:data-panel}). Volatility clustering
was also weaker in \TEST{}: the median lag-1 autocorrelation of $r_t^2$ is 0.242 in \TRAIN{}
(\cref{tab:e3-heston-moments}) and 0.061 in \TEST{}. Generators calibrated on the past are thus
judged against a calmer future, and each verdict concerns a model together with its calibration
on a window that contains the 2020 crash, not the model class alone.

Each generator gets some facts right and others wrong. GBM has neither fat tails nor clustering
(excess kurtosis 0.0 and Hill index 6.00, against 7.1 and 2.81 in the data). Merton matches the
tail index (2.73) but its \iid{} returns have no clustering. \GARCHt{}, Heston and the bootstrap
reproduce clustering but overstate it relative to \TEST{}: the median lag-1 autocorrelation of
$\abs{r_t}$ is 0.170, 0.246 and 0.169, against 0.102, and all three keep more excess kurtosis in
20-day sums (0.94, 0.84, 0.47) than the real returns ($-0.19$). The symmetric \GARCHt{} has no
leverage effect ($-0.002$ against $-0.020$), while Heston, through $\rho_v<0$, and the bootstrap
produce one. In the tails (right panel of \cref{fig:e3-stylized}) only \GARCHt{} and the
bootstrap produce moves beyond ten \TRAIN{} standard deviations, as the data do: the largest $\abs{r_{it}}/s_i$ over
tickers and days is 22.9 in the data, 24.9 for \GARCHt{} and 18.1 for the bootstrap, but 9.5
for Merton, 9.3 for Heston and 4.3 for GBM. No generator matches the \TEST{} period on every
fact, and some mismatches (volatility level, strength of clustering) are at least partly due to
the change between the two periods, which no better fit to \TRAIN{} could remove. This motivates
the anchored relative null of \cref{sec:method-relative}, which compares the
generator with the real past rather than with the future.
