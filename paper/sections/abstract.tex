Synthetic financial returns serve back-testing and portfolio construction, but their quality is
mostly judged by stylized facts or by scores without a null distribution; we cast the question
as a two-sample test. A Wasserstein GAN critic, trained alone, gives a held-out mean difference whose
permutation p-value has exact level for independent windows. For dependent panels, a time-block
split with a Driscoll--Kraay variance is asymptotically valid for a fixed critic; its simulated
size at 5\% is 0.085 when synthetic tickers share a factor but 0.270 when they are independent. An
anchored relative test, exact at its boundary for independent windows, asks whether a generator
is further than the real past from the evaluation period; a power check fixed before the full
real-data runs turns it into a verdict: \GOOD, \BAD{} or \INCONCL. In simulations, window tests
hold their level and detect dependence-only deviations but, against tail and jump deviations,
stay far below the likelihood-ratio oracle and pooled Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests (invalid under
within-window dependence). On 100 S\&P~500 stocks, GBM, Merton and Heston, fitted to 2018--2022,
are \BAD{}: too volatile for 2023--2025. GARCH-$t$ is \INCONCL{}: not detectably worse than
history, but under realistic non-stationarity the rule detects a 20\% volatility error with
probability 0.45 only. Verdicts change with the sample split. Over one evaluation period in which
correlations fell, test statistics did not predict the Markowitz utility of synthetic-data
portfolios.
