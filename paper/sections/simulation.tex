This section measures size and power where the laws that generate the data are known. In brief:
with independent windows every window test holds its level; with dependent windows the protocol of
\cref{sec:method-inference} is close to nominal when the synthetic panel shares the dependence
structure of the real one, but the learned tests over-reject when the synthetic tickers are
independent, mostly in the replications where their standard error is small; KS on pooled returns
is far more powerful than every window test against deviations of the marginal law, has no power
against deviations of the dependence alone and is invalid when returns within a window are
dependent; all tests are far below the Neyman--Pearson oracle; the anchored relative test is at
its nominal level at its boundary; and no learned score is a good estimate of the log-likelihood
ratio.

\subsection{Design}\label{sec:sim-design}

\paragraph{Independent windows (E2).}
Every base law has a mean daily log-return of $3\times10^{-4}$ and an unconditional daily standard
deviation of 0.02: \iid{} standardised Student-$t_\nu$ returns; \GARCHt{}
\citep{bollerslev1986generalized,bollerslev1987conditionally} with $a=0.08$, $b=0.90$ (persistence
$a+b=0.98$) and $\nu=5$; a Merton jump-diffusion \citep{merton1976option} with $\lambda_J=0.05$ jumps
per day and jump sizes $N(-0.01,0.04^2)$, which carry 21\% of the variance; and Gaussian GBM
returns. Every window is simulated from its own path (GARCH after a burn-in of 250 days), so the
windows are \iid{} and the permutation p-values have exact level (\cref{prop:exact-level}). The four
families of alternatives have levels $k=0,\dots,4$, with $k=0$ the null $Q=P$:
(a) \emph{volatility level}, $P=t_5$ and $Q$ the same law with volatility multiplied by
$1+\vartheta$, $\vartheta\in\{0,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3\}$;
(b) \emph{tails}, $P=t_4$ and $Q=t_\nu$, $\nu\in\{4,6,10,30,\infty\}$, at matched variance;
(c) \emph{volatility clustering}, $P$ the \GARCHt{} law above and $Q$ the same with persistence
$a+b\in\{0.98,0.95,0.90,0.80,0\}$, the same $a$, $\nu$ and unconditional variance (persistence 0 is
the \iid{} $t_5$ law);
(d) \emph{jumps}, $P$ Merton and each $Q$ window a variance-matched GBM window with probability
$\vartheta\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ and a Merton window otherwise.
Both samples have $n=m\in\{500,2000\}$ windows of $L=20$ days, with 200 replications under $H_0$ and
100 at each alternative; within a family and sample size the real sample of a replication is shared
by all levels. The tests use the defaults of \cref{tab:method-defaults} (multilayer-perceptron critic;
permutation p-values with $B=1000$ for learned and $B=500$ for kernel tests; asymptotic KS). The
oracle \eqref{eq:lr-oracle} is exact in families (a), (b) and (d); in (c) it integrates the window
density over the start variance with $M=512$ Monte Carlo draws, so it keeps its exact level but is
only approximately most powerful (\cref{sec:method-competitors}).

\paragraph{Dependence-only alternatives (E2-dep).}
Every E2 alternative changes the marginal law of a daily return, in family (c) as a by-product: at
matched unconditional variance, lower GARCH persistence gives a marginal with thinner tails. E2-dep
holds the marginal exactly fixed. $P$ is the \GARCHt{} law of family (c). In the \emph{mixture}
family a share $\vartheta\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ of the $Q$ windows consists of $L$ independent
returns, the return at position $t$ taken from position $t$ of its own independent $P$ window; the
other windows are $P$ windows. In the \emph{block} family a $Q$ window concatenates $L/L_b$ blocks
of length $L_b\in\{20,10,5,2,1\}$, each cut at its own positions from an independent $P$ window
($L_b=20$ is the null; $L_b=1$ is the law of $\vartheta=1$). This is what a block bootstrap with
blocks shorter than the memory of volatility does \citep{kunsch1989jackknife,politis1994stationary}:
every return keeps its law, and the clustering within a window is broken (diagnostics in
\cref{tab:e2dep-check}). Tests are those of E2; the oracle uses Monte Carlo densities (exact level,
approximate optimality). As run, E2-dep has $n=2000$ only, with 100 replications under $H_0$ and 50
per alternative, a compute cut from the designed $n\in\{500,2000\}$ and 200/100 replications
(\cref{app:impl}). E2-dep is not part of the original protocol: it was added after the first E2
results showed pooled KS dominating every family that changes the marginal (change log of the
protocol; \cref{tab:impl-cuts}), so it is a design chosen in view of those results; every E2
result is reported beside it.

\paragraph{Further designs.}
E2 part e draws three independent samples of $n=500$ \iid{} windows: $\Rnew$ from $\Pnew$ (the
\GARCHt{} law, persistence 0.98), $\Rpast$ from $\Ppast$ and $G$. Either $\Ppast=\Pnew$ and
$G\sim\Ppast$, or $\Ppast$ has persistence 0.95 and $G$ is drawn from $\Ppast$ (boundary), from
$\Pnew$ (interior: better than the past), from the persistence-0.90 law or GBM (worse than the
past), or is an \iid{} resample of the $\Rpast$ windows, tested once with the split grouped by
source window and once with a random split. The anchored (permutation) and two-critic (paired
bootstrap, $B=500$) tests run on the same data, 200 replications per cell; $n=2000$ was cut for
compute. Part f fits the \LRC{} logit on 2000 windows of each law, for GBM against Merton and for
persistence 0.90 against 0.98, under three training budgets (the test default with early stopping;
1000 steps without early stopping; 3000 steps at learning rate $10^{-4}$), fits the \WC{} critic on
the same data, and compares the scores with the exact $\log(p/q)$ on 2000 fresh windows, in 20
replications. Part g builds each sample from $n=500$ consecutive windows of one long \GARCHt{}
path; the two paths are independent, so $H_0$ holds, but the windows of a sample are dependent;
200 replications.

\paragraph{Simulated panel placebo (E1(a)).}
E1(a) runs the complete real-data pipeline, with the dimensions of E3, on data for which the null
holds. A one-factor panel $r_{it}=\mu_i+\beta_im_t+e_{it}$, with the equal-weight panel return
$m_t$ and the residuals following \GARCHt{} processes (\cref{sec:data-generators}), is fitted once to
the \TRAIN{} returns of the 100 tickers. Each replication simulates one stationary path of
$\Ttr+\Tte$ days and cuts it into $\Rpast^*$ and $\Rnew^*$, so that, as on real data, the last past
windows border the first new ones; $G^*$ is an independent draw of \TEST{} length. In the cell
\emph{joint}, $G^*$ is a draw of the whole factor panel; in the cell \emph{indep}, each synthetic
ticker comes from its own panel draw, so the tickers keep their marginal laws but are independent,
as for the univariate generators of E3. Windows, scaling, date ranks, split and inference are
those of E3 (3000, 3000 and 6200 windows in $\Rnew^*$, $G^*$, $\Rpast^*$); the learned tests are also
run with a ticker split, the naive protocol. The two cells share $\Rpast^*$, $\Rnew^*$ and the test
seeds; 200 replications each.

Rejection rates are reported with Wilson 95\% intervals \citep{wilson_1927_probable}, at
$\alpha=0.05$ unless stated otherwise.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Size}\label{sec:sim-size}

With independent windows every window test holds its level. Under $H_0$ at nominal 5\%, the
rejection rates of the five window tests lie between 0.025 and 0.070 over the 40 combinations of
family, sample size and test in E2, and between 0.030 and 0.080 over the 10 combinations of E2-dep;
every Wilson interval contains 0.05 (\cref{tab:e2-size,tab:e2dep-size} in \cref{app:sim}). Over the
levels 0.01, 0.05 and 0.10, 3 of the 150 intervals miss the nominal level, all at 0.01 or 0.10,
which is no more than chance produces. The tests are exact, so this checks the implementation
rather than the theory. KS is the exception: its p-value assumes \iid{} returns, and the returns of
a GARCH window share their conditional variance. In family (c) it rejects a true null at 0.110
[0.074, 0.161] with $n=500$ and 0.080 [0.050, 0.126] with $n=2000$, and in the E2-dep mixture family
at 0.100 [0.055, 0.174] (\cref{rem:ks-marginal}(ii)). With dependent windows it rejects in almost
every replication (\cref{sec:sim-dependent}).

\paragraph{Deviations of the marginal: KS wins.}
Against changes of volatility level, tails and jumps, KS on the pooled returns is far more powerful
than every window test (\cref{fig:e2-power,tab:e2-power}). It rejects a $t_6$ law against a $t_4$
law in every replication at both sample sizes, where the best window test rejects at 0.10 ($n=500$)
and 0.18 ($n=2000$). Against jumps it rejects at 0.98 at $\vartheta=0.5$ with $n=2000$, whereas no
window test exceeds 0.20 anywhere in family (d), and for $\vartheta\le0.5$ the window tests reject
at most at 0.08, about their null rates. Against a 5\% volatility increase KS rejects at 0.41
($n=500$) and always ($n=2000$); the best window test, MMD, at 0.24 and 0.93, the \WC{} test at 0.09
and 0.58. \Cref{rem:ks-marginal}(i) explains why: when returns are \iid{} within windows, the pooled
marginals are sufficient, and KS uses all $nL$ returns without splitting or learning, whereas a
window test must discover an additive function of 20 coordinates from half of the windows. A user
who cares only about the marginal law of daily returns should test it directly, with a p-value that
is valid under the dependence of the data.

\paragraph{Volatility clustering.}
At matched variance the window tests detect lower persistence, and their power grows quickly with
$n$. With $n=2000$ and persistence 0.95 against 0.98 they reject at 0.79 (\WC{}), 0.93 (MMD), 0.86
(\SigMMD{}), 0.55 (C2ST) and 0.30 (\LRC{}); at persistence 0.90 the first three reject always. With
$n=500$ the kernel tests lead (at 0.90: MMD 0.65, \WC{} 0.34). KS has higher raw rates here (0.89 at
persistence 0.95 with $n=500$), but it is oversized, its rates are not size-adjusted, and its power
comes from the change of the marginal, not from the clustering itself. E2-dep removes that channel.

\paragraph{Deviations of the dependence only.}
With the marginal fixed, KS rejects at rates between 0.02 and 0.10 at every alternative, the size
of its invalid null distribution: it has no power (\cref{rem:ks-marginal}(iii)). The window tests
detect the change (\cref{fig:e2dep-power}). In the mixture family, at $\vartheta=0.5$, 0.75 and 1,
the \WC{} test rejects at 0.46, 0.94 and 1.00, MMD at 0.70, 0.92 and 1.00, \SigMMD{} at 0.70, 0.98
and 1.00, C2ST at 0.20, 0.68 and 0.96 and the \LRC{} test at 0.14, 0.34 and 0.80. At block length
$L_b=5$ the rates are 0.86 (\WC{}), 0.78 (MMD), 0.72 (\SigMMD{}), 0.50 (C2ST) and 0.30 (\LRC{}). A
resampling generator with blocks that are too short produces exactly this kind of deviation. A
window law identifies the dependence up to lag $L-1$ (\cref{prop:window-ident}); a test of the
marginal cannot see it.

\paragraph{No window test dominates.}
A kernel test is the most powerful window test, or tied for it, at every alternative of families
(a), (c) and of the mixture family. The \WC{} test is the most powerful against the lightest-tailed
alternatives of family (b) ($\nu\in\{30,\infty\}$, and $\nu=10$ at $n=2000$), in family (d) at
$\vartheta=1$ and at $\vartheta=0.75$ with $n=2000$, where every window test is weak, and at
$L_b=5$. It rejects more often than the \LRC{} test at 20 of the 32 E2 alternatives and at all 8
E2-dep alternatives, and less often at 7 E2 alternatives, all with $n=500$ or in the jump family,
where neither rate exceeds 0.41. This comparison is between training protocols as much as between
objectives: the classifiers of the \LRC{} and C2ST tests keep the first checkpoint (step 50) in
almost every fit of families (a)--(d) (\cref{app:impl-nets}), whereas the critic always trains
for 1000 steps, so the classifier rows are a lower bound on what a logit-based test can achieve.
Training the logit for 1000 steps without early stopping improves its rank agreement with
$\log(p/q)$ only slightly (\cref{sec:sim-llr}), but the power of the classifier tests under that
budget was not measured. \Cref{rem:lr-vs-w1} therefore remains open: in population the logit is
the locally optimal direction, and with the default training protocols, at these sample sizes,
the Lipschitz-penalised critic gives the more powerful test. Power-optimised deep-kernel tests
\citep{liu2020learning} are not among the baselines.

\paragraph{Distance to the oracle.}
The oracle rejects in at least 98\% of the replications at every E2 alternative and always at every
E2-dep alternative. Every window test is far below it at the smaller deviations, and so is KS in
families (a) and (d). With a quarter of GBM windows in family (d) and $n=500$, the oracle rejects at
0.98 and the best window test at 0.08; at $\vartheta=0.25$ in the mixture family, the oracle always
and the best window test at 0.16. No test exceeds the oracle (\cref{prop:oracle-bound}; in family
(c) and in E2-dep the oracle is approximate and the bound not strict). Part of the gap is
structural: with a 50/50 split and $n=m$, the noncentrality of any test based on the held-out mean
difference of a fixed critic is at most half of the oracle's, and less when the critic's direction is not the likelihood-ratio direction
(\cref{prop:local-power}). The rest is the cost of learning. For volatility level the population
critic $\pm\norm{x}$ is nearly as efficient as the likelihood ratio ($r_f^2=0.988$ at $L=20$;
\cref{ex:vol-level}), yet the learned \WC{} test rejects a 5\% increase at 0.58 with $n=2000$,
where MMD, which neither splits nor learns, rejects at 0.93.

At the boundary $\Law(G)=\Law(\Rpast)$ the anchored test rejects at 0.065 [0.038, 0.108] when the
past and new laws coincide and at 0.055 [0.031, 0.096] when they differ
(\cref{tab:e2-relative-boundary}). Both intervals contain 0.05, as \cref{prop:anchored}(a)
guarantees for every critic and every $\Pnew$. In the interior ($G\sim\Pnew$, better than the past)
it never rejects (0.000 [0.000, 0.019]). Against worse generators its power depends on how much
worse they are: 0.865 [0.811, 0.906] against GBM, which has neither clustering nor heavy tails, but
0.135 [0.094, 0.189] against the persistence-0.90 law, which differs from the past only in
persistence; even the absolute \WC{} test separates persistence 0.98 from 0.90 with $n=500$ only at
0.34.

The two-critic test rejects at 0.040 [0.020, 0.077] and 0.065 [0.038, 0.108] at the two boundaries,
so the over-rejection predicted by \cref{prop:boundary-size} is not visible at this sample size. The
excess there is $\E\,\bar g(\abs{\delta^{\mathrm{2c}}}/\sigma_N)-\alpha$, small when the spread of
$\delta^{\mathrm{2c}}$ induced by training is small relative to the evaluation noise $\sigma_N$; the
limit $1/2$ concerns fixed training halves and growing evaluation sets. We make the anchored test
primary because its level is exact at the boundary for every critic and sample size, not because
the two-critic test failed in this design. The two-critic test is more powerful against GBM (0.960
[0.923, 0.980]) and comparable against the persistence-0.90 law (0.115 [0.078, 0.167]).

The resample rows show why a generator that copies the past cannot be judged by these tests. The
resample has the law of the past, but with a random split its evaluation windows duplicate windows
that the critic saw as pool negatives, exchangeability fails, and the anchored test rejects at 0.965
[0.930, 0.983]. Grouping the split by source window removes the leakage and makes the test
conservative (0.010 [0.003, 0.036]). In E3 the stationary bootstrap copies blocks of $\Rpast$ and is
declared \INVALID{} (\cref{sec:method-relative}, caveat (ii); \cref{rem:anchored-null}).

With balanced classes the Bayes-optimal logit is $\log(p/q)$ (\cref{prop:bayes-logit}), and the
\LRC{} statistic targets the Jeffreys divergence only if the learned logit attains it
(\cref{cor:jeffreys}). It does not at our sample sizes (\cref{tab:e2-llr}). With the test defaults,
early stopping selects the first checkpoint (step 50) and the logit is nearly constant: Spearman
correlations with $\log(p/q)$ of 0.024 (GBM against Merton) and 0.240 (GARCH), calibration slopes
$-0.002$ and 0.058. Without early stopping the rank correlations rise to 0.113 and 0.294, but the
logit becomes overconfident (GARCH: slope 2.159, root mean squared error 4.682). With 3000 steps at
learning rate $10^{-4}$ the GARCH logit is calibrated on average (slope 0.987) but noisy (Spearman
0.359). Under every budget the accuracy of the rule $\lhat>0$ lies between 0.501 and 0.553, against
0.624 and 0.619 for the Bayes rule. The \WC{} critic, whose population optimum is a Kantorovich
potential rather than $\log(p/q)$ (\cref{rem:lr-vs-w1}), ranks the GARCH windows most like the
likelihood ratio (Spearman 0.556 [0.474, 0.621]) but does no better than the logit for jumps
(0.098). In the jump case the exact log ratio is driven by the few windows that contain a large
jump (\cref{fig:e2-llr-scatter}), and no learned score tracks them; this is why every window test
has near-zero power in family (d). The learned statistics are tests, not likelihood estimators:
where a likelihood exists it should be used directly (\cref{sec:emp-likelihood}), and the critic's
role is to test with a known level where none exists and to show which windows separate the samples
(\cref{sec:emp-witness}).

\paragraph{One series.}
With consecutive windows of one GARCH path, the random-split permutation test is oversized
(\cref{tab:e2-dependent-size}): the \WC{} test rejects a true null at 0.145 [0.103, 0.200], MMD at
0.290 [0.232, 0.356] and \SigMMD{} at 0.130 [0.090, 0.184]. Neighbouring windows have correlated
critic values, which the permutation variance ignores (\cref{rem:naive-perm}), and a random split
trains the critic on windows adjacent to evaluation windows. Training on time blocks with the same
permutation p-value lowers the \WC{} rate to 0.115 [0.078, 0.167], and the Driscoll--Kraay p-value to
0.070 [0.042, 0.114]; the wild bootstrap brings MMD to 0.075 [0.046, 0.120] and \SigMMD{} to 0.090
[0.058, 0.138] (\cref{prop:hac,rem:wild}). The \LRC{} test is less affected (0.080 [0.050, 0.126]
with a random split), consistent with its nearly constant early-stopped logit.

\paragraph{The real-data pipeline.}
In the simulated factor panel (\cref{tab:e1-size-sim}), when $G^*$ is a draw of the whole panel
(cell joint) the protocol p-values are close to nominal: Driscoll--Kraay after the time-block split
gives 0.085 [0.054, 0.132] for the \WC{} test, 0.090 for the \LRC{} test, 0.075 for C2ST, 0.080 for
the anchored and 0.060 for the two-critic test; the wild bootstrap gives 0.045 [0.024, 0.083] for
MMD and 0.070 for \SigMMD{}. The small excess of the learned tests is what \cref{rem:small-T}
predicts from 15 evaluation dates and a median Bartlett lag of one window (\cref{tab:e1-sim-z});
against the fixed-$b$ reference with equal variances it disappears (\WC{} 0.050 [0.027, 0.090],
anchored 0.055 [0.031, 0.096]). The naive protocols fail. With a ticker split every training date
is also an evaluation date, and the \WC{} test rejects at 0.990 [0.964, 0.997] with the
Driscoll--Kraay p-value and always with the permutation p-value (the introduction quotes a pilot rate
of about 45\% for the latter): this is the bias of \cref{prop:ticker-bias}, which no variance
estimator removes. Under the time-block split the permutation p-value of the same critic rejects at
0.410 [0.344, 0.479]; the kernel tests with \iid{} permutation reject always and KS at 0.970.
Conditionally on their realised market paths the two panels differ, and only p-values that treat
the windows of one date as a unit (date sums, date-shared multipliers) account for this.

When the synthetic tickers are independent (cell indep), the situation of the univariate generators
of E3, the learned tests over-reject even with the time-block split: 0.270 [0.213, 0.335] for the
\WC{} test, 0.180 for the \LRC{} test, 0.115 for C2ST, 0.155 [0.111, 0.212] for the anchored and 0.190
for the two-critic test. Fixed-$b$ references do not repair this (\WC{}: 0.195 conservative, 0.245
equal-variance), while the wild-bootstrap kernel tests stay closer to nominal (MMD 0.075
[0.046, 0.120], \SigMMD{} 0.100 [0.066, 0.149]). Replication by replication, 12 replications reject
with the \WC{} test only in the joint cell and 49 only in the indep cell (exact McNemar test,
$p<10^{-5}$; \citealp{mcnemar_1947_note}). The studentised \WC{} statistic has mean 0.74 and
standard deviation 1.76 in the indep cell, against 0.11 and 1.12 in the joint cell
(\cref{tab:e1-sim-z}): it is shifted as well as over-dispersed, so the small-sample variance that
fixed-$b$ references correct is not the whole problem.

\paragraph{Where the oversize comes from.}
\Cref{tab:e1-sim-se} separates the statistic from its standard error. The raw statistic is not
more biased in the indep cell: the \WC{} statistic has mean 0.029 there and 0.026 in the joint
cell, with standard deviations 0.199 and 0.208 over replications, and the anchored statistic has
mean $-0.016$ against 0.015, although its studentised version has mean 0.33 against 0.02
(\cref{tab:e1-sim-z}). The permutation p-value of the same critic, which refers $T$ to the
spread of the pooled evaluation values and ignores the dates, rejects about equally often in the
two cells (0.410 and 0.480 for the \WC{} test, 0.400 and 0.370 for the anchored test;
\cref{tab:e1-size-sim}), and the paired differences are not significant (exact McNemar p-values
0.19 and 0.55). Nor is the average variance estimate much worse: the standard deviation of $T$
exceeds the root mean square of its standard error by a factor between 1.09 and 1.20 in the joint
cell and between 1.12 and 1.28 in the indep cell, the downward bias of a variance estimated from
few evaluation dates (\cref{rem:small-T}). What differs is the relation between the
two. In the indep cell the statistic and its standard error are negatively correlated (Spearman
correlations between $-0.54$ and $-0.40$), and the rejections concentrate where the standard error
is small: with the \WC{} test, 0.480 of the replications with a standard error at or below the
median reject and 0.060 of the others; with the anchored test, 0.270 and 0.040. In the joint cell
the correlations lie between $-0.03$ and 0.15 and the two halves reject at 0.090 and 0.080 (\WC{})
and at 0.090 and 0.070 (anchored). The shift of $\zhac$ thus reflects a dependence between $T$ and
its standard error rather than a shift of $T$: large values of $T$ come with small standard
errors, which suggests that, given the realised path of the real panel, $T$ is not centred. With
independent synthetic tickers the synthetic date sums have almost no common component, so the
standard error is driven by the real evaluation dates alone (15 for the absolute tests, 31 past
dates for the anchored test; \cref{tab:e1-sim-z}); in the joint cell the synthetic factor path,
independent of the real one, adds date-level variance that does not move with the real path.

A non-centred conditional mean is what \cref{cor:block-bias} describes for the time-block split:
in a factor model with mutually independent synthetic windows, given the factor path $F$ the
expectation of $T$ is $(\hat P_{F,\evl}-\bar Q)\fhat$, a covariance between the critic, trained on
the factor path of the training dates, and the factor path of the adjacent evaluation dates, which
need not vanish when the factor is persistent. The corollary assumes serially independent
residuals, whereas the idiosyncratic and synthetic GARCH components of the E1 panel are serially
dependent within a ticker, so reading the E1 oversize through it is model-based and heuristic
(\cref{rem:small-T}). The reading is consistent with the kernel tests, which have no training step and do not reject significantly more often in the indep cell (exact
McNemar p-values 0.26 for MMD and 0.29 for \SigMMD{}; \cref{app:sim-e1}), but we have not tested
it. The standard remedies, none of which was run, are a gap (embargo) between training and
evaluation data, as in $hv$-block cross-validation \citep{racine_2000_consistent} and purged,
embargoed cross-validation in finance \citep{lopezdeprado_2018_advances}, a contiguous split, or
a critic trained on an independent copy of the panel. An embargo bounds the remaining coupling by
the mixing coefficient at the gap (\cref{lem:decoupling}), but with about three window dates per
block every embargoed block costs evaluation dates, which are already the binding constraint
(\cref{rem:small-T}); the independent-copy critic removes the coupling entirely.

The consequence is that nominal Driscoll--Kraay p-values of the learned tests are not calibrated
for generators that simulate tickers independently. On real data the per-ticker p-values are
therefore calibrated against a simulated null, and the verdict rests on the anchored test with
the boundary size of the seed-combined rule estimated in a design that keeps the real volatility
path (design D2; \cref{sec:emp-placebo,sec:emp-power}), not on a nominal level.
